# Roanoke: The Lost Colony

**Version 0.0.5**

---

## Part One: Roanoke (1585-1603)

### The Central Conflict

The English came seeking land to own. The Powhatan lived on land they belonged to.

When the colonists traded goods for territory, they believed they had purchased exclusive rights—boundaries drawn, deeds signed, ownership transferred. But the Powhatan did not interpret "selling" land the way English purchasers understood it. To them, the land was not a commodity to be parceled and possessed. It was a living thing, shared by all who walked upon it.

When the Powhatan continued to hunt on land the English considered their possession, conflict was inevitable.

This fundamental misunderstanding—two peoples speaking the same words but meaning entirely different things—lies at the heart of Roanoke's tragedy.

---

### John White and the Secotan

Before there was a colony, there was contact.

John White arrived not as a governor, but as an artist. In 1585, he accompanied the first English expedition to Roanoke, tasked with documenting everything he saw. His watercolors captured the Secotan people in extraordinary detail—their villages, their ceremonies, their fishing weirs, their faces. He painted them not as savages, but as people. Families. A civilization.

The Secotan welcomed these strange visitors. They shared knowledge of the land: which plants healed, which waters held fish, which seasons brought abundance. Chief Wingina saw opportunity in alliance. The English had powerful tools, mysterious weapons. Perhaps they could be useful against rival tribes.

From the first interactions, both groups exchanged hostages and sent intermediaries to learn one another's languages. These interpreters became bridges between worlds—the only ones who could translate not just words, but meanings.

But White saw something the other Englishmen did not. He saw a people whose relationship with the land was ancient, intricate, and complete. They did not struggle against the wilderness—they moved within it. They did not clear forests—they shaped them. The land provided because they understood how to ask.

---

### The Voyage of 1587

In April of 1587, three ships departed Portsmouth: the *Lyon* (commanded by pilot Simon Fernandes), a fly-boat piloted by Edward Spicer, and a pinnace under Captain Edward Stafford. White had been appointed governor, with twelve assistants including his son-in-law Ananias Dare—and, strangely, Fernandes himself.

The voyage was troubled from the start.

Fernandes repeatedly undermined White's authority. In Portugal, he abandoned the fly-boat. At the Virgin Islands, he refused to let settlers gather fruit and plants. At Hispaniola, he promised to visit a friend who might provide cattle, then sailed past without stopping. At Caicos, he claimed they would find salt—they found none.

Near the Cape of Fear, Fernandes nearly ran the *Lyon* aground. Only Captain Stafford's warning saved them.

When they finally reached Hatorask on July 22nd, White intended to check on fifteen Englishmen left from a previous voyage, then continue to the Chesapeake Bay—the planned site for their permanent settlement.

Fernandes refused.

"It was too late in the summer," he declared. The settlers would disembark at Roanoke. He would take no one further except White and two or three men of his choosing.

The colonists had no choice. They landed on Roanoke—a place that was never meant to be their home.

Of the fifteen men left behind, they found only bones.

*Why did Fernandes sabotage the expedition? Was he a Spanish agent? A privateer with his own agenda? Simply incompetent? History does not record his motives—only that when the fly-boat arrived safely weeks later, Fernandes was "irritated by their safe arrival." He had hoped, it was said, that they would be killed or enslaved by pirates.*

*Whatever his reasons, his decision stranded 117 people in a place they were never meant to stay.*

---

### The First Blood (July 28, 1587)

Six days after landing, one of the twelve assistants—George Howe—went alone to hunt crabs two miles from the settlement.

The Wingino natives from Dasamonguepeuk found him there. They shot him sixteen times with arrows and crushed his skull.

It was a message, or perhaps vengeance for the violence of previous English expeditions. Either way, the colonists now knew: they were not welcome here. The fragile hope of peaceful relations had already begun to crack.

---

### The Mistaken Attack (August 8-9, 1587)

White wanted answers. He wanted justice. With twenty-three men and their Croatoan guide Manteo, he sailed at midnight to the village where Howe's killers lived.

They attacked at dawn, firing into the camp before the startled figures could flee into the reeds.

Only after the shooting stopped did they realize their mistake.

These were not the Wingino. These were Croatoan—their allies—who had come to harvest the abandoned crops of the village. The Wingino had fled after killing Howe. The Croatoan had simply arrived to claim what was left behind.

Manteo was grieved. But he placed the blame on his own people: if the Croatoan chiefs had delivered on their promise to send word, this tragedy would not have happened.

The alliance survived. But trust, once bloodied, does not easily heal.

---

### The Awkward Thanksgiving (1587)

When White returned in 1587 as governor of the new colony, he brought 117 settlers—men, women, and children. Among them was his pregnant daughter, Eleanor, and her husband Ananias Dare.

The first weeks were tense. The previous English expedition had left scars—a Secotan man killed, villages burned, trust shattered. But White believed in the power of gesture. He arranged a gathering.

They called it a feast of friendship. History would remember it differently.

The English laid out what they had: salted pork, hard biscuits, beer gone sour from the crossing. The Secotan and Croatoan brought venison, corn, squash, and fish smoked over oak. Chief Manteo came as interpreter. The elder chiefs Wanchese and the Croatoan leaders sat across from White, studying these pale strangers who could not feed themselves.

The bread was broken. Words were exchanged through Manteo, meaning lost and found and lost again in translation. The English bowed their heads and prayed to their God. The Secotan watched in silence, uncertain whether this was ritual or insult.

It was awkward. It was fragile. It was the closest these two worlds would ever come to understanding.

By the meal's end, nothing was resolved. But no blood was shed. In that time, in that place, it was enough.

---

### Virginia Dare (August 18, 1587)

Nine days after the Awkward Thanksgiving, Eleanor Dare went into labor.

The birth was difficult. The colony had no proper midwife, only women who had borne children themselves and remembered what their mothers had done. Eleanor screamed through the night while the men paced outside, useless.

At dawn, a girl was born. John White held his granddaughter in his arms—the first English child born in the Americas. They named her Virginia, for the land that was not theirs to name.

The baptism came three days later. Manteo, who had been baptized himself just weeks before, stood as witness. The colonists gathered around a makeshift font—a copper bowl the Secotan had traded for, now filled with water blessed by prayer.

Virginia Dare entered the Christian faith in a land where Christ had never walked, surrounded by people who worshipped older gods, beneath trees that had stood for centuries before England had a name.

White painted her. He painted Eleanor holding her. He painted Ananias standing proud and terrified.

These paintings would be the only proof she ever existed.

---

### White's Departure

Within weeks, the colony faced crisis. Supplies were desperately low. The Spanish threatened English shipping. Someone had to return to England for help.

White did not want to go. Every instinct told him to stay with his daughter, his granddaughter, his people. But the colonists insisted. He was the only one with connections at court, the only one who might secure the ships and supplies they needed to survive.

On August 27, 1587, nine days after Virginia's birth, John White sailed for England.

He promised to return within months.

War with Spain would keep him away for three years.

---

### The Lost Years (1587-1590)

What happened in those three years, no English record tells. But the colonists did not simply wait to die.

They adapted. They learned. The Croatoan, bound by the fragile peace of that awkward feast, taught them which roots were poison and which were medicine. How to read the sky for storms. How to fish the sounds and trap the marshes.

Some colonists married into the tribes. Children were born—English and Secotan blood mixing in veins that knew no borders. The rigid structures of English society began to soften, then dissolve.

And Virginia Dare grew.

By the time she was three, she spoke more Algonquian than English. She ran barefoot through the forests with Croatoan children. She learned to move silently, to read tracks, to understand the language of birds and wind. The land that her grandfather had tried to paint—she became part of it.

---

### Virginia's Adolescence (1590-1603)

White returned in 1590 to find the colony abandoned. But abandoned is not the same as destroyed.

The colonists had not vanished—they had dispersed. Some went south to the Croatoan. Others scattered to allied villages along the sounds and rivers. They shed their English clothes, their English names, their English God. They became something new.

Virginia Dare became something else entirely.

By her adolescence, she was no longer the governor's granddaughter. She was a child of two worlds who belonged fully to neither. The Croatoan elders recognized something in her—a gift, they said, or perhaps a curse. She could walk between. She could see what others could not.

The English colonists who remained looked to her not as a leader, but as a translator of more than words. When the spirits of the land grew restless, when the old ways and new ways ground against each other, Virginia was the one who knew how to listen.

Some said she had been touched by the land itself. That the same ground that had swallowed Roanoke had claimed her as its own.

---

### The Disappearance

The colonists did not vanish in a single night. They faded, like morning mist, like the memory of a dream.

By the early 1600s, there were no more "English" on Roanoke—only people with English blood who no longer remembered England. They had been absorbed, adopted, transformed. The word "CROATOAN" carved on that post was not a cry for help. It was a direction.

But Virginia Dare's fate was different.

The Croatoan tell a story. They say that when she came of age, she walked into the forest and did not return. Not taken. Not killed. Simply... gone. As if the land had opened up and welcomed her home.

Some say she still walks there, in the spaces between worlds. That on certain nights, when the mist rises from the sounds and the old oaks whisper, you can see a figure moving through the trees—not quite English, not quite Secotan, not quite human anymore.

The first child born of two colliding worlds became something neither world could hold.

---

### The Mystery

What happened to the Lost Colony of Roanoke?

They were never lost. They were transformed.

But the English who came after—the Jamestown settlers, the historians, the myth-makers—could not accept that. Better to imagine tragedy than to admit the truth: that some of their own had looked at English civilization and chosen something else.

The mystery endures because the answer is unbearable.

And Virginia Dare? Her fate remains the deepest mystery of all. The girl who was baptized in copper and wilderness, who grew up between two worlds, who walked into the forest and became legend.

CROATOAN.

The word carved in wood. The name of a people. The direction of a vanishing. The last trace of 117 souls who chose to disappear.

## Part Two: Jamestown (1607-1616)

### The Second Attempt

Seventeen years after White found Roanoke empty, the English tried again.

In 1607, three ships carried 104 men and boys to the shores of the Chesapeake Bay. They built a fort on a swampy peninsula along the James River and called it Jamestown. This time, the Crown was determined not to lose another colony.

But the land did not want them.

The water was brackish. Disease spread through the camp. The gentlemen among them refused to work with their hands. Within months, more than half were dead.

They survived only because the Powhatan allowed it.

---

### Wahunsonacock

The English called him "Powhatan," but that was the name of his people, not his own. He was Wahunsonacock, paramount chief of an alliance of over thirty tribes stretching across the Tidewater region.

He had heard of these English before. Rumors from the south spoke of a settlement that had appeared and vanished. Roanoke. His people had watched, waited, wondered.

Now they had returned, weaker than expected, arrogant beyond measure.

Wahunsonacock could have destroyed them. Some of his advisors urged it. But he saw another possibility. These strangers had copper, had steel, had guns. They were dying by their own incompetence. Perhaps they could be managed. Made useful. Absorbed into the Powhatan world rather than permitted to build their own.

He sent food. He sent emissaries. He watched.

---

### Pocahontas

Her true name was Amonute. In private, her family called her Matoaka. The English would remember her as Pocahontas—"the playful one."

She was perhaps eleven or twelve when Captain John Smith claimed she saved his life. Whether that story is truth, exaggeration, or fabrication, historians still debate. What is certain is that she moved between worlds with unusual ease. She visited Jamestown freely. She learned English words. She carried messages.

She was her father's daughter—watching, learning, assessing the strangers who would not leave.

---

### The Kidnapping (1613)

By 1613, the fragile peace had soured. Trade disputes, territorial skirmishes, and mutual distrust had hardened into open hostility. The English wanted leverage. Wahunsonacock had something they could take.

Captain Samuel Argall lured Pocahontas aboard his ship with the help of Japazaws, a lesser chief tempted by a copper kettle. She was taken to Henricus, a settlement upriver from Jamestown, and held as a hostage.

The ransom demand was sent to Wahunsonacock: return the English prisoners and stolen weapons, and his daughter would be released.

He returned some of what was demanded. Not all.

Pocahontas remained a captive.

---

### Rebecca (1614)

During her captivity, everything changed.

She was instructed in Christianity by Reverend Alexander Whitaker. She learned to read, to pray, to dress in English fashion. She was baptized and given a new name: Rebecca.

Whether she embraced this transformation, endured it, or performed it for survival, we cannot know. Her own voice does not survive. Only the accounts of Englishmen who saw what they wished to see.

What we know is this: in April 1614, she married John Rolfe.

Rolfe was a widower, a tobacco planter, a man who wrote to the governor confessing his love was "not with the unbridled desire of carnall affection, but for the good of this plantation, for the honour of our countrie, for the Glory of God, for my owne salvation."

The marriage brought peace. Wahunsonacock, whatever grief or calculation lay in his heart, did not strike back. For eight years, the "Peace of Pocahontas" held.

---

### What Was Lost

Pocahontas bore Rolfe a son, Thomas. In 1616, she traveled to England as a symbol of successful colonization—proof that the "savage" could be civilized.

She never saw her homeland again.

In 1617, preparing to return to Virginia, she fell ill. She died at Gravesend, perhaps twenty-one years old, and was buried in an unmarked grave beneath the chancel of St. George's Church.

Her last recorded words, spoken to her husband: "All must die. 'Tis enough that the child liveth."

## Part Three: The Unraveling (1616-1622)

### The Tobacco Boom

John Rolfe's other legacy was not peace, but profit.

He had cultivated a strain of Caribbean tobacco that grew well in Virginia soil and pleased English tastes. By 1616, tobacco had become the colony's salvation and its obsession. Every scrap of cleared land was planted. The colonists grew tobacco in the streets of Jamestown.

Tobacco demanded land. More land. Always more land.

As more plantations spread along the James River, relations with the Powhatan continued to deteriorate. Both cultures claimed use of the same earth—but their claims meant entirely different things.

The English built fences. They marked boundaries. They posted guards. The land inside those fences belonged to them, and trespassers would be met with violence.

The Powhatan continued to hunt the deer paths their ancestors had walked for generations. The deer did not recognize English fences. Neither did the people who had always followed them.

Every hunt became a provocation. Every fence became an insult. Every misunderstanding became a grievance, and grievances accumulated like dry kindling.

## Themes

### Ownership vs. Belonging
The English sought to own the land. The Powhatan belonged to it. Neither could comprehend the other's relationship with the earth beneath their feet.

### The Cost of Misunderstanding
Every conflict between settler and native stemmed not from malice, but from two worldviews so fundamentally different that common ground seemed impossible to find.

### What Survives
When a people vanish, what remains? Their words? Their blood, mixed into new generations? Their ghosts, wandering forests that no longer remember their names?

### The Artist's Eye
John White saw what others could not—or would not. His paintings preserved a world that would soon be swept away. But seeing clearly is not the same as being able to change what you see.

### Between Two Worlds
Pocahontas, the interpreters, the children of mixed blood—some people became bridges. But bridges get walked on from both sides.
